## Material source corrections

**Failure attribution / MP-Bench.** The article says three experts agreed on the causal step only 16.2% of the time and describes the best system as finding the true cause in its top five 0.44 of the time versus 0.13 for random. [MP-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.25001v1) reports a different unit: 16.2% is the share of *steps labeled as failure-inducing* by all three annotators; 56.1% were labeled by only one annotator, with disagreement reaching 60%. Its 0.4397 versus 0.1275 comparison is nDCG@5 for a model and random baseline on the hand-crafted subset, not a probability that the single true cause appears in the top five. The study is expressly built around multiple plausible perspectives on a failed trajectory. So the useful lesson is to prioritize salient and supportable causes, while preserving ambiguity where it exists; “first wrong step” is a strong diagnostic heuristic, not a universal uniquely true root-cause label.

**Token cost / Bai et al.** The article says the paper measured 3,500 tasks and found a 30-fold model-to-model cost spread on the same task. The current [paper v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.22750v3) describes 500 SWE-bench Verified instances, four independent runs per problem, and eight models. Its up-to-30x result is variation *between runs on the same task*, not a 30x cross-model spread. It separately reports model-dependent efficiency differences, including Kimi-K2 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 using more than 1.5 million additional tokens on average than GPT-5. The paper supports recording actual tokens and distinguishing run variance from model differences; the article's task count and attribution of the 30x figure are wrong.

**In-the-wild evidence / SWE-chat.** The article uses 6,000 sessions, 44.3% output survival, and 44% user pushback. Those figures match the April 2026 [v1 snapshot](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20779v1), but the paper's 44% pushback figure is per *turn*, not per session. By the article's October 10 date, [v2 had been available since October 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20779v2): it reports almost 18,000 sessions, 707 repositories, and 45.7% pushback per turn (plus 4.5% interruptions). The updated body reports 59.4% coding efficiency and 65.4% code survival in its table; its prose sometimes uses the 59.4% figure for survival, so preserve the paper's metric labels when reusing it. The article's version is stale and its unit is wrong. Even v2 is an opt-in, open-source developer sample dominated by coding sessions, so commit retention and pushback can add real-world context but do not directly grade success for general agents or durable memory.

**Reliability trend / Rabanser et al.** The article attributes a “six times faster per year” accuracy-versus-consistency rate to the reliability paper and lists a title that differs from the current arXiv record. Current [v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16666v3) is titled *Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability*, evaluates 15 models on GAIA and a verified 26-task subset of tau-bench, and says capability gains have yielded only small reliability improvements. It uses five runs per task, but its current text does not report the article's sixfold annual rate or a derivation. This looks like an unstated calculation or a citation/title mismatch, not a result that can be attributed directly to the paper. The paper explicitly lists long-horizon and stateful reliability as future work.

**AgentLens, tau-bench, multi-agent scaling, and harness costs.** The earlier report already flagged these; the supplied text makes their relevance clearer. [AgentLens](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12925v4)'s 10.7% lucky-pass result applies to a selected subset (1,815 trajectories across 47 tasks), not every one of the 2,614 collected trajectories, and the validation used seven annotators/LLM labelers, not three human annotators. [tau-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1) says pass^8 is below 25% for retail; the article's “both” domains is not supported. Its 55% failure bucket is “wrong argument or information,” broader than a correct tool with a wrong argument. The article's multi-agent scaling figures (180 configurations, -3.5% mean, 45.2% standard deviation) match [v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v1), but the paper's expanded [v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v3) existed before the article and reports 260 configurations across six benchmarks and a broad task-dependent range. [Harness Effect](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.06906v2) studies 22 tasks and six models, not 22 engineers; its authors call the result directional at that sample size.

**Other new details that are supported, with scope limits.** The article's paraphrase statistic is supported by [Mastropaolo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00438): semantically equivalent prompt changes altered Copilot's code recommendation in about 46% of cases across 892 Java methods. This is a specific code-generation study, not an estimate for every agent workflow. Its extra judge figures are also broadly supported in their domains: [AgentRewardBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942v2) reports GPT-4o judge precision 69.8% / recall 83.1%, and official rule-based checks precision 83.8% / recall 55.9%, relative to expert labels on web-agent trajectories. This is a useful complement to the article's “rules miss valid outcomes, judges invent some” point: code checks in that benchmark were stricter and missed more valid successes, while LLM judges produced more false positives. [MAST](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3) supports the article's 0.58-to-0.77 kappa comparison for an o1 failure-mode annotator without versus with few-shot examples, but that is a specific annotation pipeline with a defined taxonomy. [Agent-as-a-Judge](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10934v2) supports approximately 90% vs. 70% alignment with human consensus on its 55-task DevAI code-development benchmark; it does not establish that a general-purpose transcript judge is equally reliable.

## What is decisive for a realistic strategy comparison

The best ideas are procedural and measurable, not a claim that one strategy wins:

- Define success from external state and required/forbidden actions before running the agent. Test task solvability from the stated initial state, and include a null-agent baseline. But a null baseline is a probe, not a guarantee: the article's own full grader still passes the do-nothing agent on 6/20 refusal tasks because those tasks require no tool and the reply check only requires keywords. For refusal tasks, the policy basis or a defensible refusal behavior needs a separate check.
- Compare each strategy on paired tasks with the same model, tools, initial conditions, and harness, using a declared token/time budget. Report task-category capability and repeatability (for example pass@k and pass^k) rather than one pooled score. Three to five runs can expose ordinary variance, but cannot establish rare severe-failure rates.
- Keep full per-run traces, before/after state, intermediate handoff briefs, retries, and actual tokens/time. In the example, token totals were not stored, so model-call count stood in for cost; the article's own cost-per-pass analysis is consequently a call-count illustration. Capture actual token use for an empirical cost comparison.
- Treat each task's memory boundary as part of its specification. The article resets its database between every episode; that catches accidental dependence on other tasks and exposed t14/t17. For a strategy whose intended advantage is durable memory, isolate each arm's profile between runs but preserve that arm's memory within realistic multi-task sequences. Include cold-start and warm-state conditions and held-out tasks so remembered evaluation answers cannot leak across trials. This is an inference from the article's clean-reset finding and the reliability paper's stated long-horizon gap, not a validated universal protocol ([Rabanser et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16666v3)).
- For multi-agent or tool-routing strategies, grade the full end state and retain each intermediate message/brief so a miss can be assigned to incomplete handoff, actor behavior, or task/grader defect. Compare against a single-agent baseline at equal budget. The cited relay study is MMLU, the equal-token study is multi-hop QA, and the current scaling study spans heterogeneous benchmarks; none directly proves which strategy wins in a persistent workflow ([relay study](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26993v1), [equal-token study](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02460v2), [scaling study](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v3)).
- Use state-based checks as the hard gate and model judges as a separate, calibrated measure of response quality. The article's transcript judge agrees with the code grader on only 37/60 examples (kappa -0.24); supplying state changes raises raw agreement to 46/60 and reduces false alarms, but it still misses all 12 code-fail cases because it lacks the gold specification. A headline match between judges is not calibration. The article's proposed universal kappa floor of 0.6 is a heuristic; neither its toy run nor the cited papers establish 0.6 as a deployment threshold.
- Build a task set from field failures and human corrections, then supplement offline tests with privacy-appropriate canaries and outcome telemetry. Treat observational signals such as SWE-chat commit survival and pushback as complementary evidence, not a direct end-state oracle. SWE-chat is limited to an opted-in coding population and changed substantially between releases.

Two guardrails are especially relevant to the optional memory/context comparison. First, a resettable short task benchmark can validate state transitions and normal-run repeatability while still saying almost nothing about cross-session memory. Second, first-wrong-step labels are useful for improvement, but the MP-Bench evidence cautions against forcing one causal step when several explanations are defensible. A useful evaluation should distinguish clear state violations from uncertain causal attribution.

## Overall reading

The article's strongest contribution is its end-to-end evaluation recipe: check state and path, audit solvability, distinguish capability from reliability, preserve traces, budget-match alternatives, and validate graders. Its own example shows why these checks matter. Its weaknesses are overconfident universal language, several source/version/denominator mistakes, and proposed gates whose thresholds are not validated. The literature is informative about evaluation design, but the cited papers do not directly evaluate the target product's long-horizon memory/context strategies. The task-specific evaluation must supply that evidence.
